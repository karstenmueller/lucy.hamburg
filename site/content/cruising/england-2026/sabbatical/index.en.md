I still dream of sailing every morning, and when I wake it takes me a while to realise that I am no longer aboard. For three months I was, day and night. Home again, though everyday life has yet to catch up with me. Whatever shape it takes... first, a fresh start. I am still making sense of the experiences and encounters along the way. Here are some that meant most to me.

## A Summer of Extremes
The summer of 2026 will go down in European climate history as one of the most extreme and devastating since weather records began. Even in southern England and Brittany I had a taste of it, though neither was as hot as elsewhere. Almost 8,600 people died from the heat in England and France, and around 14,000 in Germany. Southern Europe saw the highest temperatures, while Spain and France were among the countries affected by major wildfires.
The climate crisis and its immediate effects were among the subjects I tried harder than usual to put out of my mind during my time away. For me, it was an "endless summer": glorious weather, high temperatures, little rain and no fog. Conditions in the English Channel were settled, and the high over France occasionally brought wind from the north or east.
## Sailing into the Night
There is something wonderful about sailing into the night. Ideally, all the preparations are made in daylight, the passage plan covers everything that matters, and the forecast is good. Then the sun sets, the stars appear one by one, and perhaps the moon rises too. In the morning light, the unfamiliar bay or harbour is easy to make out, and a warm bunk is not far away.
I had spent the night at anchor in Worbarrow Bay and moved round to nearby Lulworth Cove in the morning. After a few more hours' sleep, I had something to eat and prepared for the night passage. Once the anchor was up, there were the strong tidal streams off Selsey Bill to contend with. In my planning I had chosen to give it a berth of 7 nm, as I intended to cross Lyme Bay to Dartmouth anyway.
The moon made it a very bright night, and for hours we slipped along quietly and easily. The Windpilot steered Lucy, leaving me free to let my thoughts wander. A few short naps, with an alarm set, were wonderfully refreshing. Between times, I listened to a few chapters of an audiobook about British society and culture ("My dear Britain", by Annette Dittert), a pleasing companion to the experiences of the past few weeks. Before sunrise, a dolphin came very close, and a whole pod stayed with me for a long while.
Am I on course? What lies ahead in the darkness? Only a few navigational lights are visible. Gradually, more detail emerges, and eventually the buoys can be identified by their light characteristics. From a distance, you can establish only a rough sense of where things lie; as you draw nearer over the hours, the picture slowly becomes clearer. Your field of vision contracts, and straining your eyes into the darkness gets you nowhere. A handful of distant lights provide your bearings; close to the boat, little of the sea is visible in the glow of your own navigation lights. There is no sense of depth at all. Everything is black or, at best, silvered by the moon. At last, the sun brings back the colours, the surroundings take shape, and distances become apparent again. The chill of the hours before dawn gives way to the warmth of the sun. What a delight.
How glorious to enter the mouth of the River Dart early in the morning, pass Dartmouth Castle, then round a bend and see the little town of Dartmouth ahead. And to find a vacant berth on the yacht club pontoon: make fast and straight to bed? No, I spot someone with a coffee and want one too. That turns into a Full English Breakfast with two large cups of coffee. Suitably fortified, I tumble into my bunk for a few hours' sleep before moving on.
